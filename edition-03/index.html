Why this edition exists

Edition 02 found partnerships that measured what they achieved. This edition starts from the other end: the deals famous for their price. For each one I ask what the money bought, for whom, and how we know.

01 / The finding

The bigger the price, the less we’re told.

Every one of these fifteen deals is famous for a number, and in almost every case the number is a price. Only five can show what the money changed: Jordan Brand’s sales, Pixar’s purchase price, Fenty Beauty’s first sales, Nestlé’s new coffee products and Amazon’s Prime sign-ups. None has a modelled effect.

The most reliable prices didn’t come from marketing at all. Visa’s FIFA fee and Manchester United’s Chevrolet deal are confirmed in stock market filings, and Google’s $20bn payment to Apple came out in an antitrust trial. Most other values are press estimates, some from a single anonymous source.

When the results stay private, behaviour is the next best evidence. Emirates keeps renewing with Arsenal, and Disney bought the partner it once financed.

A note on scope

What Edition 03 leans on

All 15 cases carry a deal value, flagged separately so it isn’t read as a result. Most are American or global deals between 1984 and 2022. Many of the values are press estimates that the partners never confirmed, and each case says which. Not every partnership leaves a number behind. Where it doesn’t, I say so, and I rate the record for what it is.

01 Nike × Michael Jordan / 1984 / Money for associationAir JordanA deal meant to sell $3m of shoes built a brand worth billions a year.Endorsement · Signed, launched and reported $126mFirst-year sales, as widely reportedSales trend

United States, then worldwide / Sportswear and basketball

Nike and Michael Jordan, 1984

The situation

In 1984 Nike was behind in basketball, and Michael Jordan was a rookie who preferred Adidas. Nike bet heavily on one young player instead of spreading its money across many.

The exchange

Nike paid Jordan and gave him his own shoe line. Jordan gave Nike his name, his game and, over time, an entire brand.

  • Nike Money, design and a signature shoe built around one player.
  • Michael Jordan Talent, fame and, eventually, a brand bigger than the shoe.

The turning point

The Air Jordan 1 went on sale in 1985. Nike had reportedly hoped for $3m of sales in its first years, and it sold about $126m in the first year.

My take

The deal every athlete negotiation is compared to. The price was ordinary. The structure, a brand around one person, was not.

02 Disney × Pixar / 1991 / Talent for scaleThree Films, Then EverythingA three-film deal ended with Disney buying its partner for $7.4bn.Content partnership · Signed, launched and acquired $7.4bnDisney’s all-stock price for Pixar, 2006Sales trend

United States / Film and animation

Disney and Pixar

The situation

Pixar was a small computer animation company. Disney had the money, marketing and cinemas to release films around the world.

The exchange

In 1991 Disney agreed to finance and distribute three Pixar films. Pixar would deliver the finished films.

  • Disney Funding, marketing and worldwide distribution.
  • Pixar Computer animation and storytelling no studio could match.

The turning point

The deal produced Toy Story in 1995, the first fully computer-animated feature film. After disputes, including over whether Toy Story 2 counted, Disney bought Pixar in 2006.

My take

The most expensive way to end a partnership is to buy your partner. Disney decided it was cheaper than losing them.

London / Airlines and football

Emirates and Arsenal, 2004

The situation

Arsenal was building a new 60,000-seat stadium and needed money to pay for it. Emirates wanted a global brand in London.

The exchange

Emirates paid for the stadium’s name and the front of Arsenal’s shirts. Arsenal got long-term money while it paid for the stadium.

  • Emirates About £100m for naming rights and shirt sponsorship.
  • Arsenal A new stadium, a global fan base and the shirt.

The turning point

Signed in October 2004, the deal covered the stadium’s name for 15 years and the shirt for eight. The stadium opened in 2006.

My take

The renewals tell you more than the original price. Twenty years on, both sides keep choosing each other.

04 Visa × FIFA / 2007 / Money for associationThe Deal Mastercard LostFIFA swapped its payments partner, and ended up in court.Sponsorship and placement · Signed, contested and launched $180mVisa’s payments over eight years, per its SEC filing · legacy factArchival

Global / Payments and football

Visa and FIFA, 2007

The situation

Mastercard had sponsored the World Cup for years and expected to renew. FIFA negotiated with Visa at the same time.

The exchange

Visa agreed an eight-year sponsorship from 2007. FIFA got a bigger fee, and Visa got the world’s most watched tournament.

  • Visa $180m over eight years, according to its own filing.
  • FIFA Exclusive payments rights to the World Cup.

The turning point

In December 2006 a New York court ruled that FIFA had breached its agreement with Mastercard. FIFA settled with Mastercard, and Visa became the sponsor.

My take

The most reliable price in this edition came out because of a lawsuit and a share listing, not because anyone chose to share it.

Manchester United and General Motors, 2012

The situation

Manchester United was preparing to list its shares and wanted to show sponsorship growth. General Motors wanted Chevrolet to be a global brand.

Chevrolet became United’s shirt sponsor from 2014, paying roughly twice what the previous sponsor, Aon, had paid.

Signed in July 2012, the deal began with smaller payments of about $18.6m a season before Chevrolet’s name went on the shirt in 2014.

United needed the number for investors. Chevrolet bought the most visible shirt in football. Only one of them published what they got.

Under Armour and Stephen Curry, 2013

The situation

In 2013 Stephen Curry was a promising but injury-prone player. Under Armour wanted a basketball star to compete with Nike.

The Curry One launched in 2015 as Curry became the league’s most valuable player. A Curry Brand followed in 2020.

Under Armour bought the future at today’s price. Few brands are brave enough to do that.

Global / Coffee and packaged food

Nestlé and Starbucks, 2018

The situation

Starbucks sold coffee in its cafés but was less strong in supermarkets. Nestlé was the world’s biggest food company and wanted a premium coffee brand.

The exchange

Nestlé paid for a perpetual global licence to sell Starbucks packaged coffee and tea. Starbucks kept buying and roasting the beans and managing the brand.

  • Nestlé $7.15bn up front and global grocery distribution.
  • Starbucks Its brand, coffee and a $2bn packaged business.

The turning point

Agreed in May 2018, the alliance put Starbucks coffee on supermarket shelves and in capsules around the world.

My take

Starbucks sold the right to be in your kitchen and kept control of what’s in the cup. A clean split of what each side does best.

Global / Search and smartphones

Google and Apple

The situation

Most people never change the search engine their phone came with. For years Google has paid Apple to be the default in Safari, and the terms were secret.

The exchange

Google pays Apple a share of the search advertising revenue it earns from Safari. Apple makes Google the default.

  • Google Billions of dollars a year and the search engine people use.
  • Apple The default position on hundreds of millions of iPhones.

The turning point

Documents in the US government’s antitrust case against Google showed it paid Apple $20bn in 2022, and testimony revealed the share as 36%.

My take

The biggest partnership payment in this archive is a default setting. It shows what distribution is worth, and what it can cost when regulators take notice.

05 / The evidence

What a partnership can and can’t prove

Signed, launched, measured

An announcement shows a deal was signed. A launch shows it happened. Only a result shows what it achieved. Each case says which of the three the record supports.

Deal values

A reported price is an input, not an outcome. Where a value appears, it is flagged separately and marked as confirmed, estimated or rumoured.

Two sides, two results

A partnership can work for one side and not the other. Each case lists what each partner brought, and the evidence says which side the outcome belongs to.

Who reported it

Many figures come from the partners themselves or from press estimates. The source for every outcome is named, so you can judge it.

The evidence rating

Every case gets one of five ratings, strongest first: modelled effect, sales trend, survey, reach only and archival. Here a rise in sales or company value counts as a trend. A deal term, such as a stake, is marked so it isn’t read as a result.

How cases earn a place

Each partnership has a known or widely reported value and a public record. Where the value comes from a filing or a court, the case says so. Media credits identify campaign films, archive footage and reporting on the deals.
