About this archive

Partnership Turning Points is the companion to Brand Turning Points. Many of the campaigns in that archive had two names on them, so this series asks a different question: what did each side bring, what did each expect back, and how did anyone know whether it worked?

Seven editions later, it is an archive of 100 partnerships from 1928 to 2022. Every case names what each partner brought, separates the announcement from the outcome and flags deal values, so a price isn’t read as a result. The series moves from famous collaborations, in Fame first, through deals that proved and failed, to the edges of the map in Edge first.

Behind every case are two organisations and usually hundreds of people: negotiators, designers, lawyers, athletes, musicians and, in one case, a nation’s knitters. The archive is a few hours of reading built on years of their work. I hope it does them justice.

It is a selection, not a complete list. I chose each case to show something different from its angle, and there are many more partnerships worth studying. It is also not a ranking. I rate each case on the strength of its public evidence, and that rating says more about what was measured and published than about what worked. Most figures also describe one side of the deal. Read the conclusions as questions to take into a negotiation and test.

03 / The lessons

Six lessons that hold across editions

None of these belongs to one edition. Each is backed by cases from at least two angles. Choose a partnership to read it in full.

01

Back what’s already there.

The partnerships people loved most often began before the contract. The partner already wore, drank or played the product, so nobody had to pretend.

02

Bring something the other side can’t make.

Talent for scale is the second most common exchange, in 19 of the 100 cases. The strongest pair a capability with a reach that neither side could build alone.

01 H&M × Karl Lagerfeld01 LEGO × Lucasfilm03 Disney × Pixar04 Louis Vuitton × Takashi Murakami07 Safaricom × Vodafone × UK aid

03

Sponsorship can be proved, and it can break.

Money for association is the most common exchange, in 29 cases. It holds 6 of the 10 modelled effects, and nine of the fifteen failures in Edition 05. Paying to stand next to someone is the easiest deal to measure and the hardest to undo.

02 O2 × AEG02 Heineken × UEFA Champions League04 Coca-Cola × the Olympic Games05 Hertz × O.J. Simpson05 Tiger Woods × his sponsors

04

A price is not a result.

29 cases carry a deal value, flagged so it isn’t read as an outcome. The biggest sums, from Google’s payments to Apple to Microsoft’s bet on OpenAI, came with the least public evidence of what they bought.

Go where the audience already is.

From Big Brother to Roblox, the partnerships that reached people met them somewhere they already spent their time. The ones that tried to move an audience, like Mixer, struggled.

Decide how it ends before you start.

The deals that ended in court or in a warehouse rarely agreed in advance how decisions were made, or what would happen if a partner fell. Fred Perry shows the other way: it let the partner’s family decide.

06 / What’s missing

What the series doesn’t cover yet

Where the deals were made

21 cases, 21%, started in the UK or Ireland, 34, 34%, in North America and 25 were global from the start. Europe has 9, Asia 8, Latin America 2 and Africa 1.

When

63 of the 100 cases, 63%, date from 2010 or later. Only seven come from before 1990, because older deals rarely left a public record of what each side gained.

What the proof looks like

10 of 100 cases have a modelled effect, and 8 of those are in Edition 02. 26 show a sales trend, 49 are archival only, and survey evidence is the rarest, with 3.

Whose side the evidence is on

Most outcome figures describe one partner, usually the bigger brand. Few cases show what both sides gained, and the smaller partner’s result is the hardest to find.
